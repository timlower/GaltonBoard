Correlational methods describe relationships; only experimental research demonstrates cause and effect.

Three strategies, one question

Every choice in the tree answers a form of the same question: can this study say anything about cause and effect? Correlational research establishes that a relationship exists and describes it. Differential research compares existing groups. Experimental research manipulates a variable, controls others and randomly assigns participants, so it alone can demonstrate cause and effect.

Is my correlation significant?

Critical values of r

A correlation is significant when it is unlikely to occur by chance in a population with no relationship. The larger the sample, the smaller the correlation needed. Significance is not strength: use r² to judge how much variability is predicted.

Concept check

How the tree decides

  1. Manipulation. Manipulating a variable sets the direction of the relationship; random assignment then gives a true experiment. Without it the study is quasi-experimental at best.
  2. Data collected. Groups of scores point to differential research; two scores per individual to a correlation; three or more variables to multiple regression.
  3. Kinds of scores. Numerical scores use Pearson (linear) or Spearman (monotonic); ranks use Spearman; a numerical score with two categories uses the point-biserial correlation; with three or more categories it becomes a group comparison; two categorical variables use chi-square.
  4. Purpose. Describing, predicting, evaluating reliability or validity, and testing theories lead to different uses of the correlation.
  5. Selector. For numerical pairs described by a correlation, three questions score Pearson and Spearman and add cautions.

The scores are a heuristic aid. They do not replace design judgment, ethics review or the standards of your field.

Glossary

Correlation coefficient
A number from −1 to +1: its sign is the direction, its absolute value the strength or consistency.
Coefficient of determination (r²)
The proportion of the variability in one variable that is predicted by the other.
Third-variable problem
An unidentified variable may be responsible for an observed relationship.
Directionality problem
A relationship does not show which variable influences the other.
Predictor and criterion
The predictor (X) is used to predict the criterion (Y).
Monotonic relationship
A relationship in which Y moves in one direction only as X increases, not necessarily along a line.
Point-biserial correlation
A Pearson correlation in which one variable has two categories coded 0 and 1.
Chi-square test
A test of whether two non-numerical variables are related, based on counts in a matrix.

Notation

O
An observation or measurement.
X
A treatment (in the group designs) or the predictor variable (in correlation and regression).
Y
The second score, or the criterion variable.
R, NR
Random assignment, or groups formed without it.
