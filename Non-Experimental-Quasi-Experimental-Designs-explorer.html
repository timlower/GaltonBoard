Three research strategies, separated by control

Nonexperimental and quasi-experimental designs often look like experiments: they produce groups of scores that can be compared. The difference is that the groups are defined by a participant variable (for example gender, school or age) or by an event, rather than by a manipulated independent variable. What separates the strategies is the degree to which the design limits confounding variables and controls threats to internal validity.

Nonexperimental. Makes little or no attempt to minimize threats to internal validity.
Quasi-experimental. Makes some attempt to minimize threats: almost, but not quite, a true experiment. There is still no random assignment to groups and no assurance that groups are equivalent.

In this context the variable that distinguishes the groups is the quasi-independent variable, and the variable measured within each group is the dependent variable.

Describe a study

1. Does the researcher manipulate the variable of interest (create the treatment conditions)?
2. Are participants randomly assigned to conditions?
3. What is the basic structure of the comparison?
4. When is each participant measured?
5. Is a treatment or event applied to one of the groups?
6. (Developmental studies) How are ages compared?

Details

The six time-related and group-related threats in plain language

Individual differences (selection)
Groups were not created by random assignment, so they differ in participant characteristics before any treatment.
History
Outside events that occur between measurements, or only in one group (local history), change scores.
Maturation
Natural change in participants over time. In a nonequivalent-group study the groups may mature at different rates (selection by maturation).
Testing
Repeated testing produces practice or fatigue effects.
Instrumentation
The measurement procedure or instrument changes between observations.
Statistical regression
Participants selected for extreme scores tend to score closer to the mean when measured again.
Attrition
Participants drop out, and the dropouts differ from those who stay.
Cohort (generation) effects
People of different ages also belong to different generations that grew up under different conditions.

Scenario practice: is the conclusion justified?

A hidden scenario is generated at random: a preexisting-group difference, a pretest-posttest comparison, a time series, or an age comparison. Sometimes the design and data support the stated conclusion; more often a threat gets in the way. Concept check

Nonexperimental versus quasi-experimental

The two strategies are separated by the degree to which they limit confounding variables and control threats to internal validity. A nonexperimental design makes little or no attempt to minimize threats. A quasi-experimental design makes some attempt, for example by adding a control group, a pretest or a long series of observations, and is almost but not quite a true experiment. Neither manipulates an independent variable in a way that rules out all alternative explanations, so conclusions about cause and effect are tentative.

Nonequivalent-group designs (between-subjects)

Different groups of participants are formed under circumstances that do not permit the researcher to control assignment; the groups usually already exist, so random assignment is impossible. Because the groups have different participant characteristics, individual differences preclude a clear cause-and-effect explanation.

  • Differential design (ex post facto): compares preexisting groups to establish differences "after the fact". Nonexperimental.
  • Posttest-only nonequivalent control group: a treatment group and a similar but nonequivalent control group, measured once after treatment. Nonexperimental.
  • Pretest-posttest nonequivalent control group: both groups are measured before and after, but only one is treated. The pretest and the comparison group limit several threats, so it is quasi-experimental.

Pre-post designs (within-subjects)

One group is measured before and after a treatment. The threats are history, instrumentation, testing effects, maturation and statistical regression.

  • Pretest-posttest design: one observation before and one after, with no attempt to control the threats. Nonexperimental.
  • Time-series design: a series of observations before and after the treatment or event. It reveals preexisting trends and shows practice, fatigue, maturation, instrumentation and regression as changes before the treatment. Quasi-experimental.

Developmental designs

  • Cross-sectional: separate groups of different ages measured at one time. Fast and no waiting, but factors other than age (cohort or generation effects) differ among groups. It is a nonequivalent-group design.
  • Longitudinal: the same individuals measured repeatedly. No cohort effects and it assesses individual change, but it is time-consuming, attrition may bias the sample and practice effects are possible.
  • Cross-sectional longitudinal (cohort-sequential): separate samples (as in cross-sectional) obtained at different times (as in longitudinal). It can separate age from cohort and can examine the development of phenomena other than individual aging.

The group-by-time interaction in the mixed ANOVA tests whether the groups changed by different amounts, which is the quasi-experimental question. Between-subjects analyses follow chapter 8 and within-subjects analyses follow chapter 9 of the course material.

Metrics used here

Cohen's d, dz
Mean difference divided by the pooled SD (between groups) or the SD of the difference scores (paired).
Partial η²
Share of variance in an effect relative to that effect plus its error.
Difference in gains
Mean gain of the treated group minus mean gain of the comparison group: the group-by-time interaction in a 2 × 2 mixed design.
Level change and slope change
In segmented regression, the jump in the series at the treatment point relative to the projected pre-treatment trend, and the change in slope afterwards. Autocorrelation is not modeled.
Regression toward the mean
Selected extreme scorers move toward the mean on retest by (1 − reliability) times the size of the selection.
