What makes a study an experiment?

An experiment tries to establish a cause-and-effect relationship by doing four things: manipulation of one variable (the independent variable, IV, which creates the treatment conditions), measurement of a second variable (the dependent variable, DV), comparison of the scores across treatment conditions, and control of every other variable so that none can explain a difference in scores.

Directionality problem. A relationship alone does not say which variable is the cause. Manipulation solves this: the researcher sets the IV first and then watches the DV.
Third-variable problem. A hidden variable may produce the relationship. Control solves this: a variable that changes systematically with the IV is a confound. Extraneous variables that vary randomly are not a threat.

Scenario practice: is the causal claim supported?

A hidden scenario is generated at random: sometimes a real treatment effect, sometimes a confound, an uncontrolled order effect, selective attrition, contamination between groups, or simply too much noise. Concept check

Answer a few questions about your study

1. Are the treatments prone to practice, fatigue, boredom, carryover, or is the effect irreversible (e.g. learning)?
2. Can participants realistically complete every condition (time, travel, dropout risk)?
3. How large are stable individual differences in the DV compared with the treatment effect?
4. Are participants scarce or expensive?
5. Do you have a measurable variable that strongly predicts the DV (a pretest, age, ability) to match on?
6. How many treatment conditions?
7. Scale of measurement of the DV

Fit of each design

A heuristic aid, not a substitute for design judgment, ethics review or your field's standards. Designs can be combined (for example, a mixed design with a between-subjects factor and a within-subjects factor).

Between-subjects design

Each participant is tested in only one treatment condition, so separate groups of participants supply the scores in different conditions. Every score is independent of every other score. Goal: to find out whether differences exist between two or more treatment conditions.

Strengths

  • No practice, fatigue, boredom or contrast effects from earlier treatments
  • Each score is independent
  • Simple structure; suits treatments that cannot be undone

Limits

  • Needs many participants (3 conditions with 30 scores each means 90 people)
  • Individual differences can confound the study and inflate within-treatment variance
  • Groups must be created, treated and composed equivalently
  • Differential attrition and communication between groups threaten internal validity

Within-subjects design

One group of participants is observed in every treatment condition (a repeated-measures design). Each participant serves as his or her own control, so there are no individual differences between groups.

Strengths

  • Needs relatively few participants
  • Removes individual differences as a confound and reduces variance
  • Higher chance of detecting a treatment effect

Limits

  • Time-related threats: history, maturation, instrumentation, regression toward the mean
  • Order effects: practice, fatigue and carryover
  • Participant attrition across the series of treatments
  • Counterbalancing spreads order effects across treatments but does not eliminate them, and adds variance

Matched-subjects design

A separate group is used for each treatment, but each individual in one group is matched one-to-one with an individual in every other group on a variable that matters for the study (for example a pretest score). The aim is to keep the advantages of within-subjects and between-subjects designs without the disadvantages of either. It only helps as far as the matching variable predicts the DV.

Controlling extraneous variables

Hold constant
Remove the variable by limiting participants or settings. Takes extra effort and limits external validity.
Matching
Make values equivalent across conditions. Needs extra measurement and only covers measured variables.
Randomization
A random process (assignment or order) prevents a systematic relationship with the IV. Controls many variables at once, but does not guarantee balance in a given sample.

Key terms

Independent variable, levels, treatment condition
The variable the researcher manipulates; its values (levels) define the treatment conditions.
Dependent variable
The variable observed for changes that assess the effect of the IV.
Extraneous vs. confounding variable
An extraneous variable becomes a confound only if it influences the DV and varies systematically with the IV.
Experimental vs. control condition
The control condition is the no-treatment (or placebo) condition used for comparison.
Manipulation check
A direct measure of whether the IV had its intended effect on participants. Most important for participant manipulations, subtle manipulations, simulations and placebo controls.
Mundane vs. experimental realism
Simulations and field studies increase external validity but admit nature and can compromise control.

Counterbalancing

Complete counterbalancing uses every possible order of the treatments (k! sequences) with equal numbers of participants per sequence. Partial counterbalancing uses a subset; a Latin square has each treatment appear once in each position. A balanced Latin square (Williams design, k sequences for even k, 2k for odd k) also makes each treatment follow every other treatment equally often, which balances simple carryover. Counterbalancing spreads order effects evenly but does not remove them, so they still add variance. It cannot repair asymmetric order effects, where the order effect depends on which treatment came first.

Metrics used here

Cohen's d
Mean difference divided by the pooled standard deviation (between-subjects). For paired data dz divides by the SD of the difference scores.
η² and partial η²
Share of variance explained by treatments. Partial η² removes subject variance from the denominator in repeated-measures ANOVA.
F ratio
Between-treatment variance divided by within-treatment (error) variance. Large differences between treatments help; large differences within treatments hide patterns.
Standardized mean difference on a confound
Difference in mean confound between groups in SD units; values near 0 indicate equivalent groups.
Cramér's V
Effect size for chi-square, from 0 to 1.
Power
Probability of detecting a real effect at the chosen alpha. The comparison tab uses the noncentral-t approximation.

Repeated-measures ANOVA here assumes sphericity; no correction is applied.
