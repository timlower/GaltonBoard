What’s happening? A ball’s final bin is the number of times it bounced right, so it follows a binomial distribution with n = rows and p = chance of bouncing right. Dashed lines show the expected counts. As n grows and more balls drop, each histogram approaches a normal (bell) curve, illustrating the Central Limit Theorem. Board B (optional) drops from a point shifted left or right of Board A's drop point, with its own independent bounce probability, so you can compare two distributions on the same pegboard at once. When Board B is on, a third row of numbers beneath the two boards' own axes labels the shared physical position on the pegboard, the scale both boards actually land on. The mean difference, z statistic and p-value below use that shared scale: each board's sample mean is converted to a position on it (Board B's mean plus its offset), and a two-sample z-test on those positions, using each board's own sample standard deviation and ball count, tests whether the two boards' average landing positions differ by more than chance would predict. Changing any setting resets both boards.
