Factor Analysis Explorer

Ask what common causes could lie behind a set of correlated measures. Explore the structure of the data with exploratory factor analysis (extraction, how many factors, rotation), then test a stated structure with confirmatory factor analysis (fit, model comparison, modification). Four worked examples: the Five Factor Model and the HEXACO model of personality, and the factor structures of Wechsler-type and Kaufman-type intelligence batteries.

Method guide

MethodHow it worksReach for it when…Watch out for…
Principal Components (PCA)Rotates the axes to uncorrelated components ranked by variance, analysing all the variance in each item (1s on the diagonal of the correlation matrix).You want to summarise many correlated measures into a few scores and make no claim about latent causes.Not a factor model: it treats unique and error variance as signal, so loadings and communalities are inflated.
Principal Axis Factoring (PAF)Puts communality estimates on the diagonal, extracts factors, re-estimates communalities, and repeats until they settle. Only shared variance is analysed.You want a common-factor solution without distributional assumptions, or ML will not converge.No fit statistics. Communalities can drift to 1 (a Heywood case) when there are too many factors.
Maximum Likelihood EFAFinds the loadings and unique variances that best reproduce the correlation matrix under multivariate normality. Gives χ², RMSEA and BIC for each number of factors.You want to compare solutions with different numbers of factors by formal fit.The χ² test is hypersensitive in large samples; ordinal items (Likert) violate the normality assumption.
VarimaxOrthogonal rotation that makes each factor’s squared loadings as spread out (some large, many near zero) as possible, keeping factors uncorrelated.You need uncorrelated factors or a quick, easy-to-read simple structure.Forces zero factor correlation. When the true factors correlate (as in cognitive abilities) loadings smear across factors.
QuartimaxOrthogonal rotation that simplifies the rows of the loading matrix: each item loads mainly on one factor, which tends to leave a large general first factor.You expect a dominant general factor.Can hide group factors behind the general one.
PromaxStarts from varimax, raises the loadings to a power (κ) to create a target, then rotates obliquely toward it so factors may correlate.A fast oblique rotation that is easy to explain.Larger κ makes the factors more distinct but also inflates their correlations.
Oblimin (quartimin)Direct oblique rotation that minimises cross-products of squared loadings, trading simple structure against factor correlation.Factors are expected to correlate: personality traits, cognitive abilities.Solutions vary with the δ setting (fixed here at 0, quartimin). Interpret the pattern and the factor correlations together.
Parallel analysisCompares the observed eigenvalues with those from random, uncorrelated data of the same size. Keep factors whose eigenvalue beats the 95th percentile of the random ones.You need a defensible number of factors; it is usually the most accurate single rule.Based on PCA eigenvalues. With strongly correlated factors (intelligence) it can suggest fewer factors than truly exist.
Kaiser rule and screeKeep components with eigenvalue > 1, or those before the “elbow” on the scree plot.A quick first look only.The Kaiser rule over-extracts with many items and under-extracts with few; the elbow is subjective.
Velicer’s MAPExtracts components one at a time and stops when the average squared partial correlation among the items is smallest.A second opinion beside parallel analysis.Tends to under-extract when factors are weak or strongly correlated.
Confirmatory factor modelYou state in advance which items load on which factors, fix all other loadings to zero, estimate by maximum likelihood, and ask whether the implied correlations reproduce the observed ones.You have a theory, a published structure or an earlier EFA to test in new data.Good fit never proves a model true; rival models can fit equally well. Fit can also look good because the model is flexible.
Correlated vs. uncorrelated factorsCorrelated-factor models estimate every factor correlation; uncorrelated models fix them to zero.Compare them to test whether the factors really are independent.Forcing zero correlations when factors overlap produces clear misfit.
Higher-order modelFactor correlations are explained by a second-order factor (g) that influences each first-order factor.You believe a general factor sits above a set of broad abilities or traits.It implies a tidy pattern among the factor correlations; a general factor can be the effect of only some indicators.
Bifactor modelEvery item loads on a general factor and on one specific factor; all factors are uncorrelated.You want to separate general from specific variance.Very flexible, so it often wins on fit even when wrong. Check for tiny or negative specific loadings and improper solutions.
Fit indicesχ² tests exact fit. CFI and TLI compare with a no-correlation baseline; RMSEA measures misfit per degree of freedom; SRMR averages residual correlations; AIC and BIC trade fit against complexity.CFI/TLI ≥ .95, RMSEA ≤ .06 and SRMR ≤ .08 are common guides; lower AIC/BIC means a better compromise.Cut-offs are rules of thumb, not laws. Judge them together with the residuals and the theory.
Modification indicesScore tests estimating how much χ² would fall if one fixed loading were freed, plus the loading it would take.You need to find where a model is mis-specified.Specification searches capitalise on chance. Free only paths you can justify, then confirm in a new sample.

The factor model

Factor
An unobserved variable assumed to influence several measured items, so that they correlate.
λ, Λ
Loading: the effect of a factor on an item. Λ is the whole matrix (the pattern matrix). With standardized items and factors a loading behaves like a regression weight.
Φ
Correlations among the factors. Zero when the factors are uncorrelated (orthogonal).
h², u²
Communality and uniqueness. h² is the share of an item’s variance explained by the common factors; u² = 1 − h² is its unique (specific + error) variance.
Model equation
R ≈ ΛΦΛ′ + Ψ, where Ψ holds the unique variances. Estimation searches for Λ, Φ, Ψ that reproduce the observed correlations R.
Pattern vs. structure
With correlated factors, the pattern matrix holds unique effects of each factor; the structure matrix (pattern × Φ) holds item–factor correlations. This page shows the pattern.
Simple structure
Each item loads strongly on one factor and near zero on the rest. Rotation aims for it.
Cross-loading
A non-trivial loading of an item on a factor other than its main one.
Heywood case
An impossible estimate, such as a communality at or above 1 (negative unique variance). It signals too many factors, too little data or a mis-specified model.

Exploratory analysis

Eigenvalue
Variance captured by a component of the correlation matrix. Items are standardized, so one item contributes 1.
Scree plot
Eigenvalues in descending order. The elbow, or where the observed line meets the random-data line, hints at the number of factors.
MAP
Velicer’s Minimum Average Partial test: the number of components after which the average squared partial correlation among items stops falling.
KMO
Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin measure of sampling adequacy: how small the partial correlations are compared with the raw ones. Above .80 is good, below .50 unacceptable.
Bartlett’s test
Tests whether the correlation matrix is an identity matrix (no relationships at all). Nearly always significant; it only rules out the hopeless case.
Congruence (φ)
Tucker’s coefficient: similarity between an estimated factor and the true one used to simulate the data. Above .95 is essentially identical, .85–.94 fair. Known only because the data are simulated.
κ (kappa)
The power used by promax. Larger values give a cleaner but more correlated solution.

Confirmatory analysis

Identification
Factor variances are fixed to 1 so that every loading can be estimated; loadings are therefore standardized. Degrees of freedom = distinct correlations and variances (p(p+1)/2) minus estimated parameters.
χ², df, p
The likelihood-ratio test of exact fit. A small p means the model-implied correlations differ from the observed ones by more than sampling error would allow.
CFI, TLI
Comparative and Tucker–Lewis indices: improvement in fit over a baseline in which items are uncorrelated. Higher is better; ≥ .95 is a common benchmark.
RMSEA
Root mean square error of approximation: misfit per degree of freedom, adjusted for sample size. ≤ .06 is a common benchmark.
SRMR
Standardized root mean square residual: the typical size of the gaps between observed and implied correlations. ≤ .08 is a common benchmark.
AIC, BIC
Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC): each is a model’s misfit (χ²) plus a penalty for the number of estimated parameters. BIC’s penalty grows with sample size, so it favours simpler models more strongly than AIC. Only differences between models fitted to the same data mean anything; lower is better.
Residual
Observed correlation minus the correlation the model implies. Large residuals show what the model fails to explain.
MI, EPC
Modification index: the expected drop in χ² (1 df) if a fixed loading were freed. Expected parameter change: the loading it would probably take.
ω (omega)
Composite reliability: the share of variance in a sum score of a factor’s items that is due to that factor.
AVE
Average variance extracted: the mean squared standardized loading of a factor’s items.
Higher-order, γ
γ is the loading of a first-order factor on the second-order factor g. Its square is the share of that factor’s variance that g explains.
Bifactor
A model with one general factor and several specific factors, all uncorrelated, where each item loads on the general factor and one specific factor.

Personality

Five Factor Model (FFM)
The leading trait model of personality: individual differences are organised into five broad, relatively independent dimensions, often called the Big Five. Each trait is measured here by five questionnaire items.
HEXACO model
A six-factor trait model of personality, built from lexical studies in several languages. It keeps five of the Big Five dimensions (in a slightly different form) and adds Honesty-Humility. Each factor is measured here by five items.
H: Honesty-Humility
Sincerity, fairness, modesty and lack of greed; the low pole is manipulativeness and a sense of entitlement. Example item: “Would not flatter people to get ahead”. The HEXACO-only factor that the Five Factor Model has no separate scale for.
HEXACO E: Emotionality
Fearfulness, anxiety, need for emotional support and sentimentality. It differs from Neuroticism because it leaves out anger and irritability. Example item: “Wants emotional support from others”.
HEXACO X: eXtraversion
Social confidence, liveliness, cheerfulness and comfort in leading. Example item: “Enjoys lively social events”. The letter X is used so that E can stand for Emotionality.
HEXACO A: Agreeableness
Forgiveness, gentleness, flexibility and patience; the low pole is anger and holding grudges (anger sits here, not in Emotionality). Example item: “Is patient with difficult people”.
HEXACO C, O
Conscientiousness (organisation, diligence, prudence) and Openness to Experience (aesthetic appreciation, inquisitiveness, unconventionality); both closely resemble their Five Factor Model counterparts.
E: Extraversion
Sociability, assertiveness, energy and enjoyment of company. Example item: “Makes friends easily”.
A: Agreeableness
Warmth, trust, sympathy and cooperativeness toward others. Example item: “Takes time to help people”.
C: Conscientiousness
Organisation, self-discipline, dependability and goal-directed effort. Example item: “Finishes tasks on time”.
N: Neuroticism
Tendency to experience anxiety, moodiness and emotional instability; the low pole is emotional stability. Example item: “Worries a lot”.
O: Openness
Openness to experience: imagination, curiosity, and interest in ideas and the arts. Example item: “Enjoys abstract ideas”.
Reverse-keyed item
An item worded in the opposite direction of its trait (for example “Keeps in the background” for Extraversion). It loads negatively on its factor and correlates negatively with the rest of its scale.
Likert response
A rating on an ordered agreement scale, here 1 to 5. Rounding to a few categories slightly weakens correlations, and treating the ratings as continuous is an approximation.
Item codes
Each item is labelled by its trait letter and number, such as E1 to E5 or N1 to N5 (Five Factor Model), or H1 to H5 (HEXACO).

Intelligence

g (general intelligence)
The general factor behind the positive correlations among all cognitive tests. In higher-order models it sits above the broad abilities; in bifactor models every subtest loads on it directly.
Positive manifold
The finding that every cognitive test correlates positively with every other, which is the empirical basis for g.
CHC
The Cattell–Horn–Carroll theory: a widely used model of cognitive abilities with a general factor at the top, broad abilities beneath, and narrow abilities below those. Five broad abilities are used here.
Gc: Comprehension-Knowledge
Crystallised ability: breadth and depth of acquired knowledge and verbal reasoning. Subtests: Similarities, Vocabulary, Information, Comprehension.
Gf: Fluid Reasoning
Solving novel problems by inductive and deductive reasoning, independent of acquired knowledge. Subtests: Matrix Reasoning, Figure Weights, Arithmetic.
Gv: Visual Processing
Perceiving, analysing and mentally manipulating visual patterns. Subtests: Block Design, Visual Puzzles, Picture Completion.
Gwm: Working Memory
Holding information in mind and manipulating it over a few seconds. Subtests: Digit Span, Letter–Number Sequencing.
Gs: Processing Speed
Speed and fluency of simple, well-practised visual tasks under time pressure. Subtests: Symbol Search, Coding, Cancellation.
Wechsler scales
The WISC (children) and WAIS (adults) batteries. Their index scores are VCI, VSI, FRI, WMI and PSI in the five-index versions (WISC-V, WAIS-5), and VCI, PRI, WMI and PSI in the four-index versions (WISC-IV, WAIS-IV). The Wechsler-type data set uses subtests like theirs.
VSI, FRI
Visual Spatial Index and Fluid Reasoning Index: the two indexes that replace Perceptual Reasoning (PRI) in the five-index Wechsler scales. They correspond to the CHC abilities Gv and Gf.
Kaufman scales
The KABC-II (Kaufman Assessment Battery for Children, Second Edition) can be interpreted with the CHC model (five broad abilities, including Knowledge) or with Luria’s neuropsychological model (four processing scales, with no Knowledge scale). The Kaufman-type data set uses subtests like theirs.
Gsm: Sequential / Short-Term Memory
Holding and recalling information in order over a short interval. Subtests: Number Recall, Word Order.
Gv: Simultaneous / Visual Processing
Integrating many pieces of visual information at once, as in spatial and closure tasks. Subtests: Triangles, Block Counting, Rover, Gestalt Closure.
Glr: Learning / Long-Term Retrieval
Learning new associations, storing them and retrieving them later. Subtests: Atlantis, Rebus, and their delayed versions.
Gf: Planning / Fluid Reasoning
Novel problem solving and reasoning about patterns and stories. Subtests: Pattern Reasoning, Story Completion.
Gc: Knowledge / Crystallized
Acquired vocabulary and general knowledge. Subtests: Verbal Knowledge, Expressive Vocabulary, Riddles.
VCI, PRI, WMI, PSI
The four index scores of Wechsler-type batteries: Verbal Comprehension, Perceptual Reasoning, Working Memory and Processing Speed. The “four index” model groups subtests this way; Gf and Gv are combined in PRI, and Arithmetic is grouped with working memory.
Subtest
One task within a test battery. Subtests are coded here by short labels such as SIM (Similarities), VOC (Vocabulary), MR (Matrix Reasoning), BD (Block Design), DS (Digit Span), SS (Symbol Search) and CD (Coding).
Scaled score
A subtest score standardised to a mean of 10 and a standard deviation of 3. The “integer scaled scores” option rounds the simulated data this way.

All data are simulated in your browser; nothing is uploaded. Item wordings are original, and the population parameters are illustrative values in the range reported for personality questionnaires and for Wechsler-type and Kaufman-type batteries (modelled on the Cattell–Horn–Carroll framework). They are not norms for any published test. Subtest names are used descriptively.

Estimation: PAF, ML (Jöreskog) and PCA extraction; gradient-projection rotation (Bernaards & Jennrich); Horn’s parallel analysis and Velicer’s MAP; CFA by maximum likelihood with BFGS and analytic gradients. Standard errors come from the observed information, with the delta method for standardized loadings.
