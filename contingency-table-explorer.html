Then measure how strong the association is with phi and Cramér’s V, and compare every test on the same data.

Formulas used

Chi-square statistic. χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E, summed over every category or cell. p-values come from the χ² distribution (regularised incomplete gamma function).

  • Goodness of fit: E = n · weight / Σweights, df = k − 1.
  • Independence: E = (row total × column total) / n, df = (r − 1)(c − 1).
  • Yates correction (2 × 2 only): each |O − E| is reduced by 0.5 before squaring. Phi and V are always computed from the uncorrected χ².
  • Standardised residual: (O − E) / √E.

Phi (φ). For a 2 × 2 table with cells a, b (row 1) and c, d (row 2):

  • φ = (ad − bc) / √[(a+b)(c+d)(a+c)(b+d)], the Pearson r between the two 0/1 variables. Its sign depends on how the rows and columns are ordered; its size does not.
  • φ² = χ² / n, so χ² = n·φ². Effect size: φ² is the proportion of variance shared.
  • Largest possible |φ| given the margins: φmax = √[plo(1 − phi) / (phi(1 − plo))], where plo and phi are the smaller and larger of the two "second row" and "second column" proportions.

Cramér's V. V = √[χ² / (n · min(r − 1, c − 1))], which runs from 0 (no association) to 1 (perfect association). For a 2 × 2 table V = |φ|.

  • V2 = χ² / (n · df*), with df* = min(r − 1, c − 1).
  • Guidelines (Cohen): small ≈ .10/√df*, medium ≈ .30/√df*, large ≈ .50/√df*. For df* = 1 these are .10, .30 and .50.

Cohen's w (goodness of fit, and the general effect size): w = √(χ² / n). Small ≈ .10, medium ≈ .30, large ≈ .50. For a contingency table w = V·√df*, and for a 2 × 2 table w = |φ|.

Binomial test. For x successes in n independent trials with null probability p₀: P(k) = C(n, k) p₀k (1 − p₀)n−k.

  • One-sided p = P(X ≥ x) or P(X ≤ x). Two-sided p = Σ P(k) over every k with P(k) ≤ P(x). Mid-p subtracts half of P(x) from a one-sided tail (or counts half the ties in the two-sided sum).
  • Clopper–Pearson interval: the lower limit solves P(X ≥ x; p) = α/2, the upper limit solves P(X ≤ x; p) = α/2. Wilson: [p̂ + z²/2n ± z√(p̂(1−p̂)/n + z²/4n²)] / (1 + z²/n). Wald: p̂ ± z√(p̂(1−p̂)/n).
  • Cohen’s h = 2·asin√p̂ − 2·asin√p₀; small, medium, large ≈ .2, .5, .8.
  • With two categories Pearson’s χ² is (x − np₀)² / [np₀(1 − p₀)] = z², valid when np₀ and n(1 − p₀) are at least 5.

Odds ratio. With cells a, b (row 1) and c, d (row 2), the odds of the outcome in each row are a/b and c/d.

  • OR = (a/b) / (c/d) = ad / bc. OR = 1 means no association; swapping rows or columns turns OR into 1/OR.
  • Wald interval: ln OR ± zα/2 · SE, SE = √(1/a + 1/b + 1/c + 1/d), then exponentiate. Test: z = ln OR / SE. If any cell is 0, ½ is added to every cell (Haldane–Anscombe).
  • Exact (conditional) interval: the set of ψ for which the observed a is not in either α/2 tail of the noncentral hypergeometric distribution P(a) ∝ C(n₁, a) C(n₂, m₁ − a) ψa. The conditional estimate (MLE) is the ψ whose mean equals the observed a, so it differs slightly from ad/bc.
  • Relative risk RR = [a/(a+b)] / [c/(c+d)] (Wald interval on ln RR); risk difference RD = a/(a+b) − c/(c+d). For rare outcomes OR ≈ RR; for common ones OR is further from 1.
  • Effect-size labels use Cohen’s d = ln OR · √3/π, so small, medium and large correspond to OR ≈ 1.44, 2.48 and 4.27 (or their reciprocals).

Fisher’s exact test. Fix the margins (n₁ = a+b, m₁ = a+c, N). Then a is hypergeometric: P(a) = C(m₁, a) C(N − m₁, n₁ − a) / C(N, n₁).

  • Two-sided p = Σ P(k) over all k with P(k) ≤ P(observed). One-sided p = P(a ≥ observed) or P(a ≤ observed).
  • Mid-p counts half the probability of the observed table (and of any equally probable table): pmid = Σ P(k < obs) + ½ Σ P(k = obs) (two-sided).
  • Fisher–Freeman–Halton (r × c): P = (Π ri! Π cj!) / (N! Π xij!); p = Σ of P over every table with the same margins and P ≤ P(observed). Large cases use a Monte Carlo estimate.

Likelihood-ratio G². G² = 2 Σ O ln(O / E), referred to the χ² distribution with the same df as Pearson’s χ².

Why both a test and an effect size? χ² grows in proportion to n, so with enough data a trivial association is "significant". φ and V do not depend on n, so they describe how strong the association is. Everything runs in your browser; nothing is uploaded.

Key: symbols and terms
χ²
Chi-square statistic: the total gap between observed and expected counts. Bigger means a worse fit to H₀.
Σ
Sum over every category (or table cell).
O
Observed count: what the data actually show.
E
Expected count: what H₀ predicts.
(O − E)²/E
One category's contribution to χ². Squaring removes the sign; dividing by E scales the gap to the size of the count.
Residual
(O − E)/√E, the signed, standardised gap. Beyond about ±2 is notable. Orange = more than expected, blue = fewer.
n
Total number of observations.
H₀ / H₁
Null hypothesis (nothing going on) and the alternative.
df
Degrees of freedom: sets the shape of the χ² curve. k − 1 for goodness of fit, (r − 1)(c − 1) for independence.
p-value
Chance of a χ² at least this large if H₀ were true. It is the shaded area beyond your statistic, not the probability that H₀ is true.
α
Significance level: the cutoff for p, and the size of the red rejection region.
Critical value
The χ² value where the rejection region begins. Reject H₀ when your χ² is beyond it (equivalently, p < α).
H₀ weight
Relative size of each category under H₀ in a goodness-of-fit test.
Yates correction
2 × 2 only: subtracts 0.5 from each |O − E| to offset the approximation's tendency to overstate significance.
φ
Phi coefficient: the strength of association in a 2 × 2 table, equal to the Pearson r between two 0/1 variables. Ranges from −1 to +1.
φ²
Phi squared: proportion of variance in one 0/1 variable shared with the other, and χ²/n.
φmax
The largest |φ| the table's margins allow. Unequal margins make a perfect φ = 1 impossible.
V
Cramér's V: strength of association for a table of any size, from 0 (none) to 1 (perfect). It rescales χ² by n and by df*, so it is comparable across sample sizes and table shapes.
df*
min(r − 1, c − 1): the smaller table dimension minus one. Larger tables need smaller V values to count as small, medium or large.
w
Cohen's w, √(χ²/n): effect size for goodness of fit (and a general effect size for any χ²).
Binomial test
Exact test of whether the probability of success equals a hypothesised p₀, using the binomial distribution of the success count.
x, n, p̂
Number of successes, number of trials, and the observed proportion x/n.
p₀
The proportion H₀ claims is true.
Clopper–Pearson
The exact confidence interval for a proportion: guaranteed coverage, so somewhat wide.
Wilson interval
A score-based interval for a proportion that behaves well in small samples and near 0 or 1.
h
Cohen’s h: effect size for a difference between proportions, on an arcsine scale. .2 small, .5 medium, .8 large.
Odds
Events divided by non-events: a/b. A 50% risk is odds of 1; a 75% risk is odds of 3.
OR
Odds ratio: the odds in row 1 divided by the odds in row 2, ad/bc. 1 means no association; above 1 the first row has higher odds of the outcome.
ln OR, SE
The natural log of the odds ratio and its standard error. The log scale is symmetric around 0, so Wald intervals are built there and exponentiated.
Wald CI
Confidence interval from the normal approximation to ln OR. Fine for large counts, unreliable for small or zero cells.
Exact CI, conditional MLE
Interval and estimate of the odds ratio from the exact conditional (noncentral hypergeometric) distribution given the margins. Valid for small samples.
RR, RD
Relative risk (ratio of the two proportions) and risk difference (their gap).
Fisher’s exact test
A test of association that computes the probability of every table consistent with the margins, so no large-sample approximation is needed.
Hypergeometric
The distribution of one cell’s count when the margins are fixed, like drawing without replacement.
Mid-p
A Fisher p-value that counts only half the probability of the observed table, making the test less conservative.
Conservative
A test whose real false-positive rate is below its nominal α. Exact tests on discrete data usually are.
G²
Likelihood-ratio statistic, 2ΣO ln(O/E): an alternative to Pearson’s χ² with the same large-sample distribution.
Freeman–Halton
Fisher’s exact test generalised to tables larger than 2 × 2.
Haldane correction
Adding ½ to every cell so a zero cell does not make the odds ratio 0 or infinite in Wald calculations.
Type I error / power
The false-positive rate when H₀ is true, and the chance of rejecting when a real association exists.
True departure / association
How far the population being sampled is from H₀ (0 = H₀ exactly true). The population's own w, φ or V is shown under the sliders.
Margins
The row and column totals of a table.
Effect size
How large an effect is, independent of whether it is statistically significant.
Data source
Simulated data come from the sliders and presets. With My own data you paste counts, or raw cases that are tallied for you, and every table, chart and result is computed from them. The H₀ weights on the goodness-of-fit tab stay editable.
Simulation
1,000 random samples drawn assuming H₀ is true. Their χ² histogram should hug the curve; the share beyond your χ² approximates p.

Single-file, dependency-free demo. Random samples use a seeded generator. Chi-square, G² and Wald tests rely on large-sample approximations and work best when every expected count is at least 5; Fisher’s exact tests do not, but condition on the margins and are conservative. Tests assume independent observations and counts (not percentages). A significant result shows a pattern, not its cause. Exact computations are checked against SciPy.
