Every design the tree can recommend

Descriptive designs describe variables as they occur naturally; relationships are the business of correlational research and cause and effect of experimental research.

Three strategies, one question

Descriptive research describes a single variable, or each variable on its own, as it exists naturally. Correlational research establishes and describes relationships between variables, and only experimental research demonstrates cause and effect. This tree covers the first strategy and hands off to the others.

Concept check

How the tree decides

  1. Goal. Describing variables as they occur is descriptive research. Relating variables is correlational, and demonstrating cause and effect is experimental; the tree hands those off.
  2. Approach. Quantitative work turns behavior, ratings and records into numbers; qualitative work seeks understanding from language, imagery and sensory data; mixed and hybrid methods combine them; a single case can use either or both.
  3. Data source. Watching behavior leads to observational designs, existing media or archives to content analysis or archival research, self-report to surveys, and one individual to a case study. Scores already in hand call for descriptive statistics.
  4. Selectors. Four selectors refine the observation design and its scoring method, the survey administration mode, the qualitative design and the case study variant, and add cautions.
  5. Qualitative aim. Discovering theory, interpreting texts, describing essences, understanding cultures, community photography and challenging power lead to different designs.

The scores are a heuristic aid. They do not replace design judgment, ethics review or the standards of your field.

Glossary

Reactivity
A change in behavior because the person knows they are observed; related to demand characteristics.
Habituation
Repeated exposure until the observer is no longer a novel stimulus.
Inter-rater reliability
Agreement between independent observers or coders.
Frequency, duration and interval methods
Counting instances, timing how long behavior lasts, and recording behavior in each of a series of intervals.
Time, event and individual sampling
Observing selected time periods, specified events or selected individuals.
Nonresponse bias
Distortion when those who respond differ from those who do not.
Case history
A case study without any treatment or intervention.
Statistic and parameter
A summary value describing a sample, and one describing a population.
Verstehen
Understanding rather than simple knowledge, emphasized in qualitative approaches.
Bracketing
Setting aside the researcher’s presuppositions in phenomenological work.
Hermeneutic circle
Repeatedly shifting between the parts and the whole to reveal influences on interpretation.
Mixed and hybrid methods
Mixed methods combine quantitative and qualitative aspects intact; hybrid methods integrate them fluidly.

Notation

M, Mdn, Mo
Mean, median and mode.
s², s, SS
Sample variance, standard deviation and sum of squared deviations (variance = SS / (n − 1)).
κ
Cohen’s kappa, a chance-corrected agreement index.
f, n
A frequency and a sample size.

Source and use

The questions, scenarios, options and considerations follow the course content on descriptive research strategies, the descriptive statistical evaluation of data and qualitative designs, as presented in the two companion explorers. Items labeled as extensions (such as Cohen’s κ, the margin-of-error calculator and the summaries of what each qualitative design asks of the researcher) go beyond that content. Example values in the result notations are illustrative.

The recommendations are heuristics. Design logic, measurement quality and ethics review still carry the inference.
