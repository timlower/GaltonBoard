Included are how behavior is turned into numbers (frequency, duration and interval methods), how observations are sampled and checked for reliability, how Likert-type survey data behave and how nonresponse bias distorts them, and how descriptive statistics organize and summarize the scores.

What descriptive research is for

The goal of descriptive research is to describe a single variable, or to describe each individual variable when several are involved. Variables are measured as they exist naturally, and the researcher is not concerned with relationships between them. The results capture interesting, naturally occurring behavior. Three quantitative designs are covered here: the observational design, the survey design and the case study.

Sampling observations

Observing a complex situation continuously is hard. Problem 1: the observer changes the behavior

Behavior must not be disrupted or influenced by the presence of an observer (demand characteristics and reactivity). Two remedies are to conceal the observer or to habituate participants through repeated exposure until the observer is no longer a novel stimulus.

Problem 2: observations involve subjective judgment

Judgment raises the question of reliability, addressed with an inter-rater reliability method: two observers independently code the same events and their codes are compared.

Content analysis uses the techniques of behavioral observation to measure the occurrence of specific events in literature, movies, television programs or similar media. Define behavioral categories, list the words that signal each, and count. The passages are short invented examples.

Archival research: events in the past

Archival research looks at historical records to measure behaviors or events that occurred in the past. The same measurement rules apply: categories, a numerical scoring method, and multiple coders for at least part of the records to obtain inter-rater reliability. The yearly counts are simulated.

Three types of survey question

Open-ended

Introduces a topic and lets participants answer in their own words. Example: What do you like most about learning mathematics? Responses must be coded before they can be counted.

Restricted

Offers a limited number of response alternatives. Example: Which do you prefer: math, reading, or science? Responses are tallied by category.

Rating-scale

Requires choosing a numerical value on a predetermined scale. Example: “I enjoy math class” from 1 to 5. Ratings can be added and averaged.

A Likert-type scale: elementary students’ attitudes about mathematics

Respondents rate each statement from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The numerical ratings can be added and averaged and are compatible with most standard statistics. The five statements are illustrative; the original item wording is in the course content. Respondents are simulated.

Administration methods at a glance

Internet surveys: sample may not be representative. Mail surveys: nonresponse bias. Telephone surveys: interviewer bias. In-person surveys and interviews: interviewer bias with individual interviews.

Guidelines for constructing a survey

  • Demographic questions are needed to describe who responded.
  • Sensitive questions need careful placement and wording.
  • Group questions that share a topic.
  • Keep the format simple and the vocabulary and language style easy to understand.
  • Select participants to whom the questions are relevant, and consult professional help in preparing surveys and identifying participants when needed.

Concept check

Descriptive research

The goal is to describe a single variable, or each individual variable when several are involved. Variables are measured as they exist naturally; relationships between variables are not the concern.

Observational design

  • Behavioral observation systematically records behavior to describe it.
  • Problem 1: the presence of an observer may disrupt behavior (demand characteristics, reactivity). Remedies: conceal the observer, or habituate participants.
  • Problem 2: judgments are partly subjective (reliability). Remedy: inter-rater reliability.
  • Quantifying: frequency, duration and interval methods.
  • Sampling: time, event and individual sampling.
  • Content analysis and archival research use the same measurement rules.
  • Types: naturalistic, participant, contrived.

Survey design

  • Obtains a “snapshot” of a particular group at a particular time.
  • Question types: open-ended, restricted, rating-scale (Likert-type).
  • Construction: demographic questions, sensitive questions, grouped topics, simple format, easy vocabulary.
  • Participants: relevant, not too restricted, representative.
  • Administration: internet, mail, telephone, in person.

Case study design

  • In-depth study of a single individual or very small group; may include an intervention.
  • A case history has no treatment or intervention.
  • Used for rare phenomena, brain injuries and new therapies.

Descriptive statistics

  • Descriptive statistics organize, summarize and simplify results; inferential statistics generalize from samples to populations.
  • A statistic describes a sample; a parameter describes a population.
  • Mean, median and mode locate the center. Variance is the sum of squared deviations divided by n − 1; the standard deviation is its square root.
  • Nominal and ordinal data: percentages, proportions, mode.

Notation and terms

X, f
A score; the frequency of that score.
M, s, s²
Sample mean, standard deviation and variance (n − 1 in the denominator).
Reactivity
Change in behavior because the person knows they are observed.
Habituation
Repeated exposure until the observer is no longer novel.
Nonresponse bias
Distortion when those who respond differ from those who do not.

Metrics and extensions used here

Cohen’s κ
Agreement between two raters corrected for chance. The course content names inter-rater reliability without specifying an index; percent agreement and κ are shown as common examples.
Partial-interval versus whole-interval
Interval scoring is shown as “behavior occurred at any point in the interval”, one common variant.
