Visual-analysis practice

A hidden scenario is generated at random: sometimes a real intervention effect, sometimes noise, drift, an outside event, or a change that never reverses. Judge it by looking at level, trend, variability, immediacy, overlap and consistency. Decide whether the data demonstrate a functional relation, then reveal the truth.

Is a functional relation demonstrated?

Answer a few questions about your study

1. Is the target behavior likely to return toward baseline when the intervention is withdrawn?
2. Is it safe and ethical to withdraw an effective intervention?
3. Do you have 3 or more comparable baselines (participants, behaviors, or settings) that can be staggered?
4. Are you comparing two or more interventions against each other?
5. Will the behavior be shaped in steps toward a criterion (e.g. gradually raise a performance level)?

Fit of each design

A heuristic aid, not a substitute for design judgment, ethics review, or your field's standards. Designs can be combined (for instance, ABAB replicated within a multiple baseline) to strengthen internal validity.

ABAB reversal (withdrawal) design

Repeated measurement in a baseline phase (A1), an intervention phase (B1), a return to baseline (A2), and a second intervention phase (B2). Experimental control is shown when behavior changes with each introduction of the intervention and reverses when it is withdrawn. That gives three chances to show the effect: A1→B1, B1→A2, A2→B2.

Strengths

  • Strong internal validity within one participant
  • Each phase change is a replication; the effect is shown 3 times
  • Simple to run and to explain

Limits

  • Needs a behavior that reverses; learned skills usually do not
  • Withdrawing an effective intervention may be unethical or unsafe
  • Carryover and slow reversal make interpretation ambiguous
  • Ends on B, but the study spends time without treatment

Multiple-baseline design

The intervention is introduced to different tiers (participants, behaviors, or settings) at staggered times. Each tier serves as a control for the others while it waits in baseline. Experimental control is shown when each tier changes only after, and soon after, its own intervention starts.

Strengths

  • No withdrawal needed, so it suits non-reversible behavior
  • Replication across tiers and across time
  • Ethical when withdrawal is not acceptable

Limits

  • Needs 3+ comparable, ideally independent, tiers (WWC: at least 6 phases)
  • Spillover (generalization) between tiers blurs the logic
  • Long baselines for late tiers can be hard to justify
  • History events hitting all tiers at once are a threat

Visual analysis: six features

Level
The mean (or median) within a phase. Compare adjacent phases.
Trend
The slope of the data within a phase. A baseline already improving weakens any claim.
Variability
Spread around level or trend. High variability makes change harder to see.
Immediacy
How fast the change shows after a phase change (this tool compares the last 3 and first 3 points).
Overlap
How much data in adjacent phases fall in the same range. Less overlap means a clearer effect.
Consistency
Whether similar phases (A1 and A2, B1 and B2) look alike, and each change goes the expected way.

Metrics used here

PND
Percent of intervention points beyond the most extreme baseline point in the therapeutic direction. Easy, but depends on one data point.
PEM
Percent of intervention points beyond the baseline median.
NAP
Nonoverlap of All Pairs: the share of (baseline, intervention) pairs where the intervention point is better, ties counting 0.5. 0.5 = chance, 1.0 = no overlap. Tau shown here is 2·NAP−1; it is not baseline-trend-corrected Tau-U.
d (within-case)
Mean difference divided by the pooled SD of the two phases. With autocorrelated data, treat it as descriptive.
Randomization test
Only valid if the phase-change points were randomly chosen before data collection. The p-value is the share of possible phase arrangements giving a statistic at least as extreme as the one observed. The smallest possible p is 1 / number of arrangements.

Design-standards reference points

The design check uses commonly cited What Works Clearinghouse single-case pilot standards (Kratochwill et al., 2010, 2013): an ABAB design needs at least 4 phases and at least 3 attempts to demonstrate an effect; a multiple-baseline design needs at least 6 phases; phases need at least 5 data points to meet standards, or 3 to 4 to meet with reservations. Also required: systematic manipulation of the independent variable and interobserver agreement collected on at least 20% of sessions in each phase. Standards are revised over time, so confirm against the current WWC handbook.

Extra conditions: C, D and E

Choose 2 to 5 conditions. A is always baseline; B to E are treatments or treatment variations. In the ABAB tab each added condition is appended as a return to baseline followed by the new condition (A B A B A C A D A E), so every treatment is compared with its own adjacent baseline. In the multiple-baseline tab each tier receives B, then C, D and E in sequence after its staggered start.

Because the conditions run in a fixed order, differences between treatments are confounded with order, time and carryover. Read treatment-versus-treatment comparisons as descriptive, and use an alternating-treatments or counterbalanced design when a head-to-head comparison is the main question.
