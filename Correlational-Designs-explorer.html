Correlational, differential and experimental research

The goal of the correlational strategy is to establish that a relationship exists between variables and to describe the nature of that relationship. There is no attempt to manipulate, control or interfere with the variables, so relationships can be described but not explained. Correlational research looks for patterns within the scores of individuals; differential research examines the scores of groups; and only experimental research demonstrates a cause-and-effect relationship.

Describe a study

1. Does the researcher manipulate a variable (create the treatment conditions)?
2. Are participants randomly assigned to conditions?
3. What data were collected?
4. What kinds of scores are X and Y?
5. What form do you expect the relationship to take?
6. What is the main purpose of the study?

Interpreting strength and significance

Two questions are different: how strong is the relationship (r and the coefficient of determination r²), and could it be chance (statistical significance)? Benchmarks: small r = .10 (r² = 1%), medium r = .30 (r² = 9%), large r = .50 (r² = 25%).

Concept check

The correlational strategy

The goal is to establish that a relationship exists between variables and to describe its nature. Variables are not manipulated, controlled or interfered with, so relationships can be described but not explained.

  • Correlational: shows that a relationship exists between two variables; looks for patterns within the scores of individuals. Does not determine cause and effect.
  • Differential: two groups of scores, focusing on the difference between groups. Does not determine cause and effect.
  • Experimental: demonstrates a cause-and-effect relationship between two variables.

The data

Each individual contributes a pair of scores, X and Y. The data can be listed (two scores per individual) or shown in a scatter plot in which each individual is a dot with a horizontal coordinate (X) and a vertical coordinate (Y).

A correlation coefficient measures and describes a relationship in three ways:

  • Direction: positive (variables change in the same direction) or negative (opposite directions); shown by the sign.
  • Form: linear (points cluster around a straight line; Pearson correlation, for interval or ratio scores) or monotonic (a one-directional trend; Spearman correlation, for ranks).
  • Consistency or strength: the numerical value, from 0.0 to 1.0 in absolute value.

Interpreting strength

The coefficient of determination (r²) is the squared correlation: the percentage of variability in one variable that is determined, or predicted, by its relationship with the other.

Degree of relationshiprr²
Small.10.01 (1%)
Medium.30.09 (9%)
Large.50.25 (25%)

A correlation is statistically significant when it is unlikely to have been produced by random variation. With a small sample it is possible to obtain a very strong-looking correlation when there is no relationship; increasing the sample size makes it more likely that a correlation represents a real relationship.

Applications

  • Prediction: a relationship lets researchers use one variable (the predictor) to predict or explain the other (the criterion). Regression finds the equation that gives the most accurate predictions of Y for each value of X. The predictor is usually simple and well defined; the criterion is relatively complex and unknown.
  • Reliability and validity: both are commonly defined by relationships established with the correlational design.
  • Theory: correlational designs can address theoretical questions, for example correlating the IQs of identical twins.

Weaknesses

  • Cannot assess causality, so internal validity is low.
  • Third-variable problem: a third variable may produce the relationship between the two measured variables.
  • Directionality problem: a correlation does not show which variable influences the other.
  • More than two variables: multiple regression can statistically control other variables, but predictors only predict.

Strengths: describes relationships, is nonintrusive (natural behavior) and has high external validity.

Notation and terms

X, Y
The two scores obtained from each individual.
r, ρ
Sample correlation; population correlation.
SP, SS
Sum of products of deviations; sum of squared deviations.
Ŷ
The value of Y predicted from X by the regression equation.
Monotonic
A relationship that goes in one direction only (always increasing or always decreasing), not necessarily along a straight line.

Metrics and extensions used here

95% CI for ρ
Fisher z interval around the sample correlation.
Partial correlation
The correlation between X and Y once the linear influence of Z has been removed from both.
Standard error of estimate
Typical size of prediction errors around the regression line; the prediction band is ±1.96 times this value (an approximation).
