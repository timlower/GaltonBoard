Factors and factorial designs

A factor is an independent variable (IV) in an experiment, especially in a study that includes two or more IVs. A factorial design includes two or more factors. A single-factor design has one IV; a two-factor design has two. The number of levels of each factor is written as a product: a 2 × 3 × 2 design has three factors and 2 × 3 × 2 = 12 conditions.

Research strategies in factorial studies

Experimental. Both factors are true independent variables manipulated by the researcher.
Nonexperimental or quasi-experimental. Most or all factors are nonmanipulated, quasi-independent variables. They are still called factors.
Combined strategies. One factor is a true IV (experimental strategy) and the other is a quasi-independent variable: a preexisting participant characteristic, or time.
Pretest-posttest control group. Treatment versus control is a between-subjects factor and pretest versus posttest is a within-subjects factor, so the study is a two-factor mixed design. The Treatment × Time interaction is the effect of interest.

Why add a second factor?

Factorial designs are used to expand and replicate earlier studies, to reduce variance in between-subjects designs, and to evaluate order effects in within-subjects designs.

Replication and expansion. Replication repeats a previous study using the same factor exactly as it was used before. Expansion adds a second factor, in the form of new conditions or new participant characteristics, to find out whether previously reported effects generalize to new situations or populations. An interaction means the effect does not generalize equally.
Why this matters. If the original effect holds at every level of the new factor, the study has expanded the evidence (a main effect and no interaction). If the effect changes, the new factor qualifies the original conclusion (an interaction).

Reducing variance in between-subjects designs

A participant variable as a second factor

Treatment groups differ because of the treatment, but participants within each group also differ on a variable such as ability. Treating that variable as a second factor removes its variation from the error term. The sample, and so external validity, is unchanged.

Treatment order as a second factor

Concept check

Main effects

The main effect of a factor is the set of mean differences among its levels, averaged over the levels of the other factor. A two-factor study has two main effects, one for each factor. The data can be structured to produce main effects for both factors but no interaction (parallel lines).

Interactions

An interaction occurs when one factor has a direct influence on the effect of a second factor. Equivalent definitions: the effects of one factor depend on the level of the other; nonparallel lines in a graph of the cell means; differences that change from one row (or column) of the data matrix to another. A statistical test decides whether an apparent interaction is reliable. A drug interaction is a familiar example: one drug can exaggerate, minimize or completely block the effect of another.

Interpreting main effects and interactions

  • A main effect is an average, so it may not accurately represent any of the individual effects used to compute it. When an interaction is significant, examine the simple effects.
  • The two main effects and the interaction are three separate and independent sets of mean differences: any combination can occur.
  • Independent factors have no interaction.

Types of factorial design

  • Between-subjects: requires many participants; individual differences can become confounding variables; avoids order effects.
  • Within-subjects: requires one group of participants and eliminates individual differences, but each participant undergoes many treatments, which is time consuming, contributes to attrition and raises the risk of testing effects.
  • Mixed: combines a between-subjects factor with a within-subjects factor; used when one factor is expected to threaten validity.

Strategies

  • Experimental: all factors are true independent variables.
  • Nonexperimental or quasi-experimental: most or all factors are quasi-independent variables.
  • Combined: one true IV plus a participant characteristic or time. A pretest-posttest control group study is a two-factor mixed design with the interaction as the effect of interest.

Metrics used here

Partial η²
Share of variance for an effect relative to that effect plus its error term.
Simple effect
The effect of one factor at a single level of the other, tested against the pooled within-cell error.
Marginal (overall) mean
The mean of a row or column of cell means; the quantity compared in a main effect.
Power
Here, the share of 300 simulated studies in which an effect was significant at α = .05.
