Experimental Designs Decision Tree

Answer a short series of questions to find the experimental research design that fits your study. The tree separates group studies from N-of-1 studies, one factor from several, manipulated from nonmanipulated variables, and random from nonrandom assignment. It then shifts to focused selectors for between-subjects versus within-subjects choices, factorial designs, N-of-1 designs and developmental designs. Each result shows the notation, the main threats, the usual analysis and the general considerations that go with it.

Every design the tree can recommend

The research strategies range from nonexperimental (little or no attempt to limit threats) to experimental (manipulation, control and random assignment, or replication within a case).

Three strategies, one question

Every choice in the tree answers a form of the same question: how well can this design rule out explanations other than the treatment? Manipulation determines the direction of the relationship, control (holding constant, matching and randomization) addresses third variables, and comparison conditions or replication show what would have happened without the treatment.

Counterbalancing: how many orders?

Complete counterbalancing uses every possible order, so it quickly becomes impractical. A Latin square uses k selected sequences in which each treatment appears once in each position. It balances carryover from the immediately preceding treatment when k is even (for odd k, two squares, 2k rows, are needed). Neither fixes asymmetrical order effects.

How the tree decides

  1. Unit of study. Group comparisons use many participants; N-of-1 or single-case designs observe one participant (or a few) repeatedly across phases.
  2. Number of independent variables. Two or more factors make a factorial design.
  3. Manipulation. Manipulating the IV sets the direction of the relationship; leaving it unmanipulated limits conclusions to differences and relationships.
  4. Random assignment. Randomization (or randomized order) gives a true experiment; without it, the best available design is quasi-experimental.
  5. Selectors. Where several designs remain, a short set of questions scores them and the highest-scoring design is recommended.

The scores are a heuristic aid. They do not replace design judgment, ethics review or the standards of your field.

Notation

O
An observation or measurement of the dependent variable.
X
A treatment or intervention.
R
Random assignment to groups or orders.
NR
Groups formed without random assignment (nonequivalent groups).
A, B
In N-of-1 or single-case work, baseline (A) and treatment (B) phases. In factorial work, the two factors.

Glossary

Confounding variable
An extraneous variable that influences the DV and varies systematically with the IV.
Third-variable problem
An unidentified variable may be responsible for an observed relationship.
Directionality problem
A relationship does not show which variable is the cause.
Counterbalancing
Presenting treatments in different orders to spread order effects evenly across conditions.
Nonequivalent control group
A comparison group formed without random assignment.
Cohort effect
A difference caused by belonging to a generation, confounded with age in cross-sectional studies.
Functional relation
In N-of-1 or single-case research, a demonstrated dependence of the behavior on the intervention, shown by replication.
