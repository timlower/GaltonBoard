Ethnography: understanding people in their own settings

Ethnographers seek greater understanding of the social relations, customs and lived experiences of various peoples and situations. Field research is typified by participatory observation, and researchers make both external and internal observations. The more the researcher participates, the more of the setting’s lived meaning becomes accessible.

Grounded theory: from codes to a theory grounded in the data

Developed by Glaser and Strauss in sociology in the late 1960s and extended to psychology, grounded theory seeks to discover underlying theories through inductive methods. It involves coding, memoing to connect and elaborate codes, theory development, and theoretical sampling to “ground” theoretical advances in the data itself. It can be used by both positivist and constructivist researchers. The excerpts below are invented interview fragments about adapting to a new workplace system.

The hermeneutic circle

Hermeneutics developed in antiquity and into the Renaissance as a means of interpreting texts, particularly religious texts, and was applied to the social sciences through twentieth-century philosophers such as Heidegger (Being and Time) and Gadamer (Truth and Method). It involves repeatedly shifting the focus of investigation between the parts and the whole of the phenomenon, or the thing and its context. This iteration reveals the various influences on interpretation; isolating them can bring later interpretations closer to the thing itself.

Phenomenology: bracketing and reduction

Phenomenology focuses on understanding conscious experience as lived intuitively and on delineating its essences. All conscious experiences and events are co-constituted by the observer and the observed. The design was introduced in philosophy by Husserl; the variety most predominant in psychology is Amedeo Giorgi’s descriptive phenomenology, which uses bracketing and phenomenological reduction to identify the essences of an experience.

Photovoice: community members as the photographers

Photovoice is a Community-Based Participatory Research design developed by Wang and Burris in the late twentieth century, derived from Wendy Ewald’s photographic work with children in Appalachia. Cultural participants capture visual imagery in order to (1) record and reflect their community’s strengths and concerns, (2) promote critical dialogue and knowledge about important issues through large and small group discussion of the photographs, and (3) reach policymakers. The eight scenes are invented illustrations, not real photographs.

Liberating methods

Often inspired in part by Paulo Freire’s Pedagogy of the Oppressed, these methods developed during the latter half of the twentieth century and embrace power/knowledge paradigms. They seek to challenge fundamental assumptions in existing knowledge systems and often integrate advocacy and activism. Qualitative strategies in general acknowledge the role of knowledge in reflecting, supporting and sustaining current power structures.

The one-line orientations are brief, general summaries added for context; consult the primary literature for each tradition.

Mixed and hybrid methods

Projects that combine quantitative and qualitative research are increasingly popular. Those that combine aspects of both approaches intact are mixed-methods approaches; those that fluidly integrate aspects of the two are hybrid methods.
